What does rising cash burn mean for AI stocks?

Last week on October 29, Alphabet had its worst day on record. Shares fell 12%, wiping out $500 billion of market value, after the company reported its second straight quarter of negative free cash flow.

Amazon fell about 7% the same week, Meta dropped 7% the week before, and the Nasdaq finished October down 5.3%, its worst month since March 2025.

Why?

For three years, investors cheered every increase in AI spending. Last week, for the first time ever, they punished it.

  • What happened: Democrats took both chambers on November 3; stocks dipped, then rebounded November 4.
  • How it impacts investors: Analysts mostly expect gridlock, which may limit deficit spending and help keep yields in check.

AI stocks are spending more than they earn

Tech sold off broadly on AI spending. Alphabet, Amazon, Meta and Microsoft plan over $740 billion of capex this year, and with Oracle, combined capex was set to exceed their operating cash around Q3.

Free cash flow shrank across the group:

  • Alphabet: negative $9.1 billion.
  • Amazon: negative $42.4 billion over 12 months.
  • Meta: negative $2.1 billion, a first since 2012.
  • Oracle: $28.5 billion capex on $19.3 billion revenue.
  • Microsoft: positive $18.6 billion.

Microsoft’s lower capex partly reflected supplier delays. The author’s view: the market is pricing AI’s cash needs, not rejecting AI.

Companies and investors are reminded that growth isn't free

The article compares the AI spenders with slower growers such as Costco, Walmart and Coca-Cola.

AI spenders grow about 21% yet trade at 20.7x forward earnings; slow growers manage about 5% at 29.6x. The author offers two readings, capital efficiency or a temporary safety premium, and notes the slow growers were chosen for trading above the AI spenders (see this Costco Narrative). The real question is whether returns arrive.

Here's what it looks like in the bigger picture

The gap is funded with debt: the five spenders raised $326 billion this year, and AI took 82.7% of US venture money. The author says this squeezes others in three ways:

First, borrowing competition. AI-related bond issuance is a record $419 billion.

Second, pricier money. Goldman links AI investment and government borrowing to a higher cost of capital; the 10-year yield peaked at 5.31% and is now 4.84%.

Third, shifting attention. Non-AI startups and smaller listed companies get overlooked.

Indebted firms look most exposed; sound low-debt firms may be left cheap.

What would tell you the flow for AI stocks is turning?

Signals the article flags:

  • Whether free cash flow tracks forecasts of about $640 billion by 2030.
  • How much spending is borrowed, and at what rates.
  • Pressure on Oracle, CoreWeave and Nebius.
  • Flows into havens like bonds and Walmart.

The Anthropic IPO, filed October 26, is another test.

The Insight: How much AI stocks spend to grow matters as much as how fast they grow

The author suggests asking of any holding: is growth self-funded or borrowed, does it profit from money flows or compete for them, and what cash flow does the price assume? The article doesn’t call AI a bubble; it says growth’s cost is now being priced.
